Read claims

Before a headline tells you AI took a job, ask what it actually measured

The verbs matter. “Exposed,” “using,” “spending,” “hiring less,” “could cut jobs” and “caused a layoff” are different claims with different evidence burdens.

Claims that get blurred together

If a source says…It measured…It did not automatically measure…
“This occupation is exposed to AI.”Tasks that a system might affect or perform.Layoffs, unemployment or a worker’s personal risk.
“Workers are using AI.”Reported or observed use of a system for work or a task.Whether employment, wages or hours changed because of it.
“A company advertised a GenAI job.”A signal that the company may be adopting or building AI capability.The exact start or intensity of use, or a staffing outcome on its own.
“Firms are spending on AI.”A purchase or spending proxy for adoption.What every worker does with the system, or whether the spending caused later employment changes.
“Hiring or pay fell in exposed work.”An observed outcome associated with exposure or adoption.That AI caused the fall, unless the comparison design rules out credible alternatives.
“Pay rose in AI-exposed jobs.”It may be posted pay, new-hire pay, incumbent pay or an average; these can move differently.That every worker got a raise, or that AI caused the change.
“AI will eliminate millions of jobs.”Usually a conditional scenario, forecast or public claim.An observed labor-market result today, or a probability that the stated future will occur.

Worked example: a hiring rating is not a hiring decision

A U.S. survey randomly framed student AI use as helpful or as a substitute for learning, then asked hiring professionals to score fictional graduates. A strong warning lowered stated interview and salary-negotiation ratings. No real application or pay was observed. Read the survey experiment before treating that rating as a job-loss count.

Worked example: more interviews are not more jobs

A London experiment found an AI-related certificate on matched fictitious applications increased pooled interview invitations for underrepresented women. It did not measure offers, hires or wages, and it cannot tell AI-specific skills from broader digital literacy. Read the study before treating a course as an employment guarantee.

Worked example: slower hiring is not mass layoffs

A Danish firm-record study reports that first-time AI adopters, especially smaller firms, hired less than their prior trend predicted. They still grew, and the study did not randomly assign adoption. A separate Danish worker study found no extra fall in early-career share at chatbot-encouraging workplaces through 2024. Different samples and outcomes prevent a simple reversal claim. Read the firm study.

Worked example: an AI job ad is not a pay rise

A Federal Reserve note finds that U.S. manufacturing ads asking for AI skills offer about 70% higher average pay than manufacturing ads overall. The ad groups differ in occupation, employer and location, and not every ad states pay. The note does not show what anyone was paid after hiring. It also finds explicit GenAI skills in under 1% of manufacturing ads, despite broader AI skills appearing more often. Ask which skill, job and wage are being counted. Read the factory update.

Worked example: changing job-ad text is not a layoff

A U.S. study scores tasks listed in online ads for potential GenAI assistance. About 39% of the post-2023 decline in its exposure index came from different listed tasks within comparable role groups. That is not 39% of workers replaced. The study cannot show that a specific employer rewrote a job after adopting AI, or that anyone was hired or fired. Read the posting update.

Worked example: more startups are not more jobs for every worker

A CEPR abstract reports 20% more startup formation and entry-linked industry labor growth in more exposed industries. A separate U.S. startup study finds that new firms offset job losses at existing exposed startups, leaving aggregate startup employment unchanged in its sample; a UK study detects no clear entry increase. These different measures are not a direct replication. A firm count is not a job count or proof of AI causation. Ask whether a study includes new-firm jobs, incumbent losses and later worker pay. Read the startup update.

Worked example: exposed work does not mean the firm adopted AI

A Census study tested 14 exposure measures against reported AI use among matched single-location U.S. firms. Exposure was positively related to use, yet explained only about 1–6% of its cross-firm variation. That is not a 1–6% chance that your firm uses AI. The paper measured adoption, not layoffs. Ask whether a labor study observed employer use or inferred it from tasks. Read the measurement update.

Worked example: a vacancy is not a hire

A U.S. software study found roughly 14–15% fewer junior ads relative to senior ads after ChatGPT’s release. It measured posted openings and experience requirements, not completed hires. The wider tech slowdown coincided with the change, and employer AI use was not observed. Ask which stage a headline counts before turning it into a job-loss claim. Read the vacancy update.

Worked example: a platform's users are not the workforce

A May 2026 preprint held a U.S. employment design fixed and changed only which AI-platform conversation shares went into its exposure score. The employment coefficient changed size and sometimes sign. After workforce reweighting, tested magnitudes fell by 42–93%. That is a warning about a proxy, not evidence that AI caused or prevented a job loss. Ask whose work is missing from the platform and whether the result survives another measure. Read the measurement update.

Five checks before you act on a claim

  1. What outcome was measured: hiring, employment, unemployment, wages, hours, task content—or a model output?
  2. Who and where: age, occupation, country, sector and period?
  3. What AI technology, exposure score or adoption measure was used?
  4. Compared with what: before and after, another occupation, another firm, a matched group or alternative model assumptions?
  5. What else could explain the difference or make the forecast fail?

A useful study can still leave your personal answer unknown. Population evidence can identify a risk pattern; it cannot calculate your odds or prescribe a career move.

Worked example: an application test is not a hiring result

In a controlled exercise, ChatGPT-assisted pitches made relevant experience harder to judge on average, but helped evaluators in a non-English-speaking subgroup. That study measured screening accuracy, not interviews, offers or employment. Another cover-letter study reports no increase in interview invitations despite better writing; its modeled matching loss is not a measured jobs loss. Ask which hiring stage a claim reaches before treating it as a job-loss finding. Read the screening update.

Worked example: higher advertised pay can accompany fewer junior openings

Indeed finds faster advertised-pay growth in highly AI-exposed U.S. occupations, but their entry-level share of postings fell sharply while senior roles took a larger share. That does not mean AI raised workers’ wages or caused a junior-job loss. The source measures job ads and occupational exposure, not employer rollout, accepted pay or employment.

A Cleveland Fed paper now adds realized pay: in more LLM-exposed U.S. occupations, posted pay rose while wages for workers who stayed fell in the later comparison period. The causal role of AI remains unproven. Read the wage update.

Worked example: a lower junior share is not automatically fewer junior jobs

Stanford’s new study finds that AI-adopting multinational affiliates had a lower junior share and higher senior employment than matched controls. Its estimated junior headcount decline was not precise, and total employment was not clearly lower. The finding is a changed workforce mix—an important warning about entry routes, not a layoff count.

Worked example: a national null is not a personal forecast

Yale Budget Lab’s September update finds no clear AI footprint in its broad U.S. measures through August. That is evidence against the claim that AI has already caused an economy-wide jobs collapse. It does not settle new-graduate hiring, a Texas posting result, one employer’s plan or your own job risk.

A broad result earns a broad conclusion. A narrow result earns a narrow conclusion. Do not let either become a career instruction.

Worked example: a 2030 number can still be a scenario

A new CEPR paper illustrates a “substantial change” path in which cognitive employment is 4% lower by 2030. The authors say median U.S. survey expectations are consistent with that path. It is not a count of people laid off in 2026, and it does not say that the path is certain.

Compare that with the Danish study: it observes slower recruitment after some firms reported AI adoption. One source is a conditional future; the other is an observed association. Neither tells you your personal odds. Read the study cards.
