AI job-risk watch · updated 14 September 2026

A tougher warning for AI-exposed graduates — not a proven job apocalypse

A new U.S. Census working paper finds weaker initial employment and pay for graduates from the most AI-exposed majors after ChatGPT's release. This is a serious early-career signal. It is not proof that AI caused every loss: the study assigns exposure rather than observes employer adoption, and it cannot rule out every field-specific shock.

What the evidence says now

FindingWhat was measuredWhat it cannot prove
High-exposure graduates had worse first outcomes. A Census paper links records for roughly 6.7 million bachelor’s graduates. The most-exposed major decile had a five-point lower initial-employment rate and 13% lower full-quarter initial earnings after ChatGPT's release than the bottom six deciles. That AI alone caused the gap. Exposure is not adoption, and work-from-home or another time-varying shock could still be part of the story.
Earlier hiring data point in the same direction. A separate Census paper finds 22- to 24-year-olds were hired less often in the most AI-exposed U.S. industry-state cells after ChatGPT's launch. That every exposed young worker, industry or employer lost work because of AI.
Broad job postings do not show the same distinct decline. A Federal Reserve analysis tests whether U.S. postings in AI-exposed occupations fell disproportionately after ChatGPT and finds little indication of a distinct AI-driven drop. What happened to actual hires, graduates or workers entering through channels that postings do not capture.
AI use is broad, but not yet deep in most work. U.S. task-level survey data find at least one in five workers use AI in more than 80% of occupations, while most occupations and tasks remain below half adoption. That an exposed occupation, or a worker who uses AI, has had an employment or wage change.

The danger line

Entry routes can narrow before an occupation disappears. The new graduate study adds an earnings outcome to the earlier hiring warning, and it finds movement toward lower-paying sectors. That is a reason to watch new entrants closely, not a license to declare a whole labor market lost.

The broad posting result is the brake on that story. Different populations and outcomes can diverge. The next question is why.

Exposure, adoption and job loss are different claims

The ILO's global index estimates potential task exposure, not layoffs. New task-adoption data make the boundary clearer: people doing similar work can use AI very differently. A capability score cannot calculate your odds of being replaced.
