AI job-risk watch · checked 24 October 2026

Young workers in exposed U.S. jobs saw another payroll decline

September’s Stanford–ADP update shows employment down 3.7% from a year earlier for ages 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations, versus 0.2% growth in the least-exposed. It does not show that AI caused those losses.

Latest check

In the selected ADP payroll panel, high-exposure employment fell 0.5% year over year across ages versus 1.0% growth in least-exposed work. For ages 26–30, the split was −2.8% versus +0.9%. The panel is not nationally representative; its occupation score measures possible AI reach, not an employer’s use.

A four-country study finds that remote-work exposure weakens the GenAI link to lower junior hiring shares through 2025. French permanent-hiring records show a later 21–29-year-old entry gap but also cannot isolate AI. German full-time records through 2025 show only a small, statistically uncertain adjusted young-worker exposure link. Yale’s broad U.S. tracker finds no clear economy-wide AI footprint. The sources count different outcomes and populations.

New: a hiring judgment, not a hiring count

A U.S. experiment found that when hiring professionals read an AI-as-substitute warning, they rated fictional new graduates less favorably for interviews and salary talks. That message changed stated judgments; the study did not observe real callbacks or pay.

The answer in one minute

Entry routes may be narrowing in exposed work, but no source here isolates how many jobs AI removed. Remote work, changing demand and employer choices complicate the attribution. Neither a weak AI coefficient in one study nor a strong French hiring gap predicts your own outcome.

Before acting on a job-loss claim

  1. Did it count hires, employment or layoffs—or only a changing share?
  2. Did it observe workplace AI use, or infer it from an occupation score?
  3. Did it compare remote work and other concurrent changes?

A population result cannot show that buying a course will protect your job.
