Research update · 21 September 2026

AI support raised job ads in Sweden. The study did not find a headcount effect.

A Swedish grant program gives the evidence map a rarer test: firms that won support for a first-time AI project later posted more vacancies than applicant firms that did not win. Five years later, their total employment, hiring and separations showed no statistically detectable change.

What this study measured

Hellsten, Khanna, Lodefalk and Yakymovych study Vinnova grants awarded in 2019–2020 to small- and mid-sized Swedish firms investing in AI for the first time. Their final sample contains 53 awardees and 137 non-awarded applicants. The study combines near-universal Swedish Public Employment Service vacancy data from 2017–2024 with population registers and uses synthetic difference-in-differences to align prior outcome paths.

This is stronger than an occupational exposure comparison because the treatment is a grant decision among firms that applied for AI-project support. It is still a grant effect, not proof that a named system caused a result at every recipient.

The result is more recruitment effort, not measured job growth

Five years after award, treated firms were 24 percentage points more likely to post a vacancy. They were 22.5 points more likely to post a white-collar vacancy and posted about 5.4 more white-collar vacancies than the comparison firms. The paper reports no statistically detectable effect on total employment, hires or separations.

That is a warning against two bad headlines. “AI grants create jobs” overreads postings as completed employment. “AI removes jobs” ignores the measured increase in recruitment effort. The study finds a real change in labor demand, but not a net headcount verdict.

Why Sweden cannot settle the wider AI jobs argument

The intervention was funding for first-time AI projects, largely before the mass rollout of generative-AI chatbots. It cannot identify the effect of a particular AI tool, tell whether each project succeeded, or show how a U.S. firm would respond. The final sample is small, and synthetic difference-in-differences cannot rule out every changing difference between winners and non-winners.

Sweden also had severe matching frictions in the period. More vacancy posting may fail to become hiring for reasons that are specific to that labor market. A vacancy is not a completed hire, and a firm-level average cannot forecast an individual career.

The conflict is not a tie; it is a map of different mechanisms

The Swedish grant result differs from Denmark’s slower recruitment at firms that reported AI use. It also differs from U.S. early-career exposure studies that find relative setbacks, and from broad U.S. posting results that find little distinct AI decline. The papers vary in country, AI measure, period, firm selection and outcome. They do not add up to a universal AI jobs score.

The strongest update is narrower: adoption support can change what firms try to hire for before it produces a measured employment change. That leaves open whether effects later appear in hires, job quality, wages, separations or different countries.

What changes in the cumulative answer

The map now contains a closer-to-causal study that breaks the simple AI-layoff story without proving the opposite. A Swedish AI-project grant increased vacancy posting, especially in white-collar roles, while leaving headcount, hiring and separations statistically unresolved. Read the outcome label before you let a job headline change your plans.

Source: Hellsten, Khanna, Lodefalk and Yakymovych, “The Effects of Artificial Intelligence on Jobs: Evidence from an AI Subsidy Program,” IZA Discussion Paper 18267, November 2025.
