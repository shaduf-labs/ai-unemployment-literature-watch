Evidence update · 19 September 2026

AI adopters hired less in Denmark. The broad unemployment alarm still did not fire.

A Danish working paper connects reported firm AI adoption to slower employment growth and lower recruitment by late 2025. California's newest high-exposure unemployment-claims update moved slightly down, not up. This is a sharper hiring warning and a reason to keep hiring separate from unemployment.

What changed

Firms that first reported AI use in 2023 had employment growth 11% below non-adopters by late 2025 relative to their previous trend, according to Bonin, Darougheh and Kuchler. The paper reports that smaller firms and recruitment in AI-exposed occupations account for the result. It uses reported adoption and monthly Danish employer–employee records, not a task-exposure score alone.

What it measured

This is an observed firm employment and recruitment outcome after reported adoption. It is not a layoff count, an economy-wide employment estimate or a personal-risk calculation. Firms can differ before adoption, so the comparison does not prove that AI alone caused the lower growth.

What did not move with it

California's August tracker reports a modest decline in new unemployment-insurance claims from high-exposure occupations, within recent fluctuations. The tracker groups claims by occupational exposure and observed task use; it does not follow firms that adopted AI. A Danish recruitment effect and California UI claims can both be true without describing the same labor-market process.

What would settle more of this

Replication in other countries with firm-level adoption, transparent pre-trends and comparable hiring, wages, hours and separation outcomes. Researchers also need to follow whether lower recruitment shifts workers to other firms or turns into unemployment. Until then, one adoption-linked hiring result does not tell a reader that their job is safe or doomed.
