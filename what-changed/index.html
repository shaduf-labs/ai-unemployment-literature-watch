Evidence update · 21 September 2026

A Swedish AI grant created more job ads. It did not create a detected headcount effect.

A closer-to-causal test of AI support changes the evidence map. Swedish firms that won funding for a first-time AI project later posted more vacancies than similar applicants that were not funded. Their total employment did not show a statistically detectable change.

What the study measured

Hellsten, Khanna, Lodefalk and Yakymovych compare 53 small- and mid-sized Swedish firms that received Vinnova AI-project grants in 2019–2020 with 137 applicant firms that did not. They use synthetic difference-in-differences, Swedish vacancy records and employment registers through 2024.

The change is in vacancy posting

Five years after award, funded firms were 24 percentage points more likely to post a vacancy. They were 22.5 points more likely to post a white-collar vacancy. The study finds no statistically detectable effect on total employment, hiring or separations.

That matters because a vacancy headline can sound like job creation, just as a vacancy decline can sound like layoffs. Neither shortcut is safe. A posted opening is an attempt to recruit; it must still become a hire before it is an employment gain.

What the grant result cannot settle

This is a causal estimate of receiving an AI-project grant among Swedish applicants, not a clean experiment assigning one AI product or generative AI to firms. The program mostly predates broad chatbot use. Sweden’s skill shortages and labor-market institutions also shape whether vacancies become hires.

Why it does not cancel the Danish warning

A Danish study follows firms that reported AI use and finds slower recruitment at some adopters. The Swedish study follows grant award and finds more vacancy posting without a detected headcount effect. Different countries, treatments, periods and outcomes prevent either paper from overriding the other. Together, they make one point harder to ignore: “AI affects jobs” is not one measurable outcome.
