Evidence update · 25 September 2026

AI-exposed work posted faster pay growth. Its entry-level share shrank.

Indeed’s U.S. posting analysis adds a new kind of warning: a job market can offer higher advertised pay in AI-exposed work while reserving a larger share of openings for senior people. It does not observe employer AI use or prove the cause.

What the analysis measured

Indeed Hiring Lab examined millions of U.S. job ads that included a salary. It classified occupations using its generative-AI skill-transformation score and compared posted pay and stated seniority over time. The study measures advertisements, not accepted wages, employment or layoffs.

What changed in the evidence map

Advertised pay in the most AI-exposed occupations increased about 46% from 2021 to mid-2026, compared with 25% in the least-exposed group. At the same time, entry-level ads in the most-exposed group dropped from 29% to 10% of its postings, while senior ads increased from 22% to 47%.

Why this is not a simple wage win

The post-ChatGPT exposure-linked pay premium is smaller once the analysis holds seniority mix constant, and that estimate is not statistically significant. A role advertised at a higher salary is not proof of higher worker earnings or an AI-caused gain. The exposure score also does not show which employer deployed AI.

Why it matters for the entry-level warning

Texas and Census records separately show lower early-career employment or earnings in more-exposed fields. The new posting result does not replicate those studies, but it explains why average pay is the wrong all-clear test: a market can tilt toward fewer, more senior openings. Yale’s broad U.S. tracker still finds no clear economy-wide AI footprint. The evidence supports a narrower warning about opportunity structure, not a mass-unemployment claim.
