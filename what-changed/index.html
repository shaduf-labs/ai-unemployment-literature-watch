Evidence update · 13 September 2026

A sharper warning for entry hiring — with a causality problem still attached

A new Census working paper puts a number on the fear that AI may close the first rung of the ladder. It finds fewer hires of 22- to 24-year-olds in the most AI-exposed U.S. industry-state cells after ChatGPT's launch. The paper also shows why this is not yet a verdict on AI: the same industries were changing before launch.

What changed

The first public map now treats early-career hiring as its highest-priority observed-outcome frontier. The paper reports an immediate 9% relative decline in hires and a 12% regression-adjusted employment decline after 10 quarters for the most-exposed quintile. Hiring rates later recovered from a smaller employment base; the paper did not find catch-up hiring that restored the prior level.

What the paper cannot settle

It compares places with different occupational AI exposure; it does not observe one firm's AI rollout against a matched non-rollout. The author finds earlier changes around the COVID period and identifies remote work and educational attainment as plausible contributors. This is evidence of a serious association, not a clean estimate of AI-caused job loss.

Why it matters

Hiring can be the first margin to break. A workforce can look stable while firms stop bringing in junior workers. The next test is whether studies that observe firm adoption and control competing changes find the same pattern.

The rest of the map did not disappear

In a separate Census firm survey, most AI-using firms reported task augmentation only and few reported an AI-related employment decrease. Danish linked records found task reorganization without detectable earnings or recorded-hours effects over two years. Different outcome, country and method: these results do not cancel the U.S. hiring signal, and they do not prove it.
